# The Regiment Württemberg and the von Franquemonts
**(First Approach to a Family History)**

**Werner Kraus**

### The von Franquemont Family

The surname Franquemont, or corrupted as "Prankemont," was widespread in the Indo-European world of Java in the 19th century. It traces back to at least five officers of the so-called "Regiment Württemberg" or "Cape Regiment," which Duke Carl II Eugen of Württemberg (r. 1737-1793) rented to the VOC (Dutch East India Company) in 1787. The regiment, led by Colonel Theobald von Hügel, consisted of two battalions stationed at the Cape of Good Hope to defend it against English attacks. While the Commander-in-Chief, Colonel von Hügel, was simultaneously the head of the 1st Battalion, the 2nd was led by Lieutenant Colonel Friedrich Wilhelm von Franquemont. He was the first of a whole series of illegitimate sons of Duke Carl Eugen of Württemberg, whom the Duke—as a 16-year-old!—had fathered in 1744 with the ducal "silver maid" Margarete Traub.

Like many of his men, Friedrich Wilhelm von Franquemont died at the Cape, specifically on December 10, 1790. At that time, the Württemberg Regiment was already in a process of creeping dissolution. In 1789, part of it was transferred to Celebes to suppress a local rebellion. Other units were sent to Ceylon, Ambon, and Java, or fell into English captivity and were interned in Madras, India.

The part of the regiment that ended up in the East Indies was soon caught in the vortex of the decline of both the VOC and the Duchy of Württemberg. Both institutions were shattered by the Napoleonic claim to rule over Europe. The newly formed Batavian Republic had neither the time nor the inclination to worry about a scattered regiment on the other side of the world, and the Duchy of Württemberg was thrust from its late-Baroque, sensual complacency into the turmoil of a fracturing Europe. In Stuttgart, too, no one wanted to worry about a regiment that was disintegrating, scattered in distant "India," without orders and without pay. Since no appeals to the distant Dukes bore fruit and no one took responsibility, the officers and soldiers of the regiment had to provide for their own advancement. Incidentally, they did not manage this too badly. A whole series of them either stood on their own feet or took a position with the Dutch (later British) administration.

The remnants of the Regiment Württemberg were finally dissolved in Java in 1808 by Marshal Daendels, the representative of the Dutch King by Napoleon's grace. Of the original 2,000 men, about 100 returned to Germany. The rest remained in Java or had long since died. One of the men of the regiment best known in German cultural history was Karl August von Wolzogen, who had studied with Friedrich Schiller at the Hohe Karlsschule in Stuttgart and would later become his brother-in-law. Wolzogen, who remained in correspondence with Friedrich Schiller while in Java, died in 1808 as Inspector of the Javanese Forestry Service in Semarang.

### Letter from Mr. Karl von Wolzogen to Friedrich Schiller

**Semarang, October 6, 1790**

It remains a true spiritual refreshment for me to read your *Don Carlos*. In addition, I possess the first edition of *Intrigue and Love*, *The Robbers*, and *The Ghost-Seer*.

How I wish to possess everything that comes from your masterful hand. In my solitary garden, where I have built a balcony covered with a dome, I shall no longer seek food for the spirit if I have your further writings with me.

On one side, a quietly flowing, rather broad river, whose banks, as far as I can see, are bordered with fragrant hedges; a large field grown with rice and sugar cane; two brickworks—all this enclosed by handsome, eternally green hills, whose peaks are covered with large tombs that are mostly dilapidated and have the appearance of ruined castles in Germany. Then high mountain peaks tower out of the clouds, among which one clearly notices one of the fire-spewing mountains by its smoke. At my back, I am covered from the sun's rays by a grove of coconut trees, which until 11 o'clock let nothing through but the playing flame-fire of the sun stealing through the feathered and fanned leaves. It is here where I dream myself away with all leisure into Germany's enlightened midst and forget, for a few mornings a week, that I am distant from my fatherland. Increase this bliss, this illusion—it shall be reckoned to you as a meritorious deed.

*(Seemann 2000)*

The last commander of the Regiment Württemberg was Franz Philipp von Winckelmann from Meiningen in Thuringia. He remained in Java after the dissolution of the troop, serving first the Bonapartist Dutch royal house under Daendels, then the English under Raffles, and finally the re-established Dutch administration after 1816. He rose to become the Head of Forestry on Java. Other officers and men of the dissolved Regiment Württemberg also made their fortune in Java. Captain Lieutenant Friedrich von Jett, for instance, remained in military service and was one of the "Curators of the Military School" in Java in 1820, i.e., one of the directors of the military academy.

As already mentioned, a number of officers of the Regiment Württemberg bore the name Franquemont. All of the von Franquemonts were direct but illegitimate descendants of Duke Carl Eugen of Württemberg (1728-1793). The Duke named his illegitimate sons not only after a remote location of his lands but also preferentially shipped them off to distant countries. Incidentally, many an honorable man of the 19th century did the same, e.g., the philosopher G.W.F. Hegel. He sent his illegitimate son and ne'er-do-well Ludwig Fischer to Java, where he died of fever in 1825, shortly after his arrival in Batavia.

Duke Carl Eugen, who was married twice and had no surviving descendants from either marriage, maintained a whole series of extramarital relationships. The children from this rapid succession of female acquaintances were all named von Franquemont or von Ostheim. According to the memoirs of Baroness Oberkirch, Carl Eugen had the plan to sire so many Franquemonts that he could have equipped the officer corps of an entire regiment with them. He was well on his way to doing so. Although he could not or would not legitimize his many children, the ducal father at least took care of them to the extent that he endowed them with a respectable name, a standing, and a profession (military). The name Franquemont referred to the small lordship of Franquemont on the left bank of the Rhine in the County of Montbéliard, which had to be ceded to France in the French-Württemberg Treaty of 1802, along with all Württemberg territories on the left bank of the Rhine (e.g., also Ostheim). *(Seemann 2000: 50-2)*

One of the officers, Friedrich von Franquemont (1770-1842), who had endured the African and East Indian hardships, managed to return to Württemberg towards the end of the 18th century. There, as an officer of the Württemberg army, he participated in all Napoleonic campaigns, was an advisor to King Friedrich I Wilhelm Carl (who was simultaneously his cousin) at the Congress of Vienna, and even became Minister of War under King Wilhelm I Friedrich Carl.

A second, Friedrich Wilhelm von Franquemont, had, as previously mentioned, died at the Cape in 1790.

A third, fourth, and fifth von Franquemont, all three officers of the Regiment Württemberg, were to settle in Java after the dissolution of the troop. These were the half-brothers **Carl David von Franquemont**, **Carl Borel von Franquemont**, and **Philipp August David von Franquemont**.

**Carl David von Franquemont** reached Batavia with part of the Regiment Württemberg on September 5, 1791. However, the troop did not stay long in the city and was transferred just a month later, on October 8, 1791, to Point Galle on Ceylon. On September 2, 1795, the Württemberg partial regiment on Ceylon was taken prisoner by the English and interned in Madras. The officers were evidently spared this fate; they do not appear on the list of internees. Carl David von Franquemont likely remained on Ceylon and fathered his son Carl Wilhelm (born around 1794) and daughter Charlotte Elisabeth (born 1797) there. A few years later, Carl David von Franquemont can be found on a "muster roll" of the Regiment Württemberg, created on April 8, 1800, in Semarang: as *Captain* and commander of the 93-man troop.

We do not know what became of his marriage to N. von Hügel. The first wife likely died on Ceylon, where the daughter Charlotte Elisabeth von Franquemont also grew up. Charlotte Elisabeth did not arrive in Batavia until April 6, 1814—coming from Point Galle on the brig "Sophia"—to marry her cousin Matteys there. By this time, Carl David von Franquemont had long been married to Louise Sophie Henriette von Jett (1783-1852), daughter of the Captain Lieutenant of the "Regiment Württemberg," Friedrich von Jett.

From this marriage, there were likely 2 children:
1.  Philipp Wilhelm von Franquemont, born around 1799, died 1829, Batavia
2.  Karoline Luise, born 1800 Java, died 1857 Ludwigsburg, married to Johann von Raben

At the end of his life, Carl David could look back on a successful military career. After the dissolution of the "Regiment Württemberg," he first became Military Commander of Surabaya and later even Vice-Commander of Weltevreden.

This was one of the most important military posts in Java. After all, the largest part of the European inhabitants of Java lived in Weltevreden, and their safety had the highest priority, for one ultimately wanted to live truly "well-satisfied" (*Weltevreden*) there.

The family of the illegitimate son of the Württemberg Duke Carl II Eugen, Carl David von Franquemont, thus managed to brilliantly master the difficult situation in which the officers and men of the Regiment Württemberg found themselves after its dissolution. They quickly conquered the highest posts in the administration and played a significant role during the complicated time of the war against Diponegoro and the establishment of the Cultivation System (forced cultivation) of agricultural export goods on Java. However, the male line of this part of the Franquemonts seems to have broken off with the death of Louis. I have so far been unable to prove marriages or children for the two brothers.

**Carl Borel von Franquemont**, born May 17, 1770, in Ludwigsburg (mother: Teresa Bonafini), died April 29, 1811, in Batavia. He was also an officer in the Cape Regiment and shared the fate of the other brothers. In March 1793, he married the daughter of the Regimental Commander Colonel Maximilian von Hügel, Baroness Maria Barbara von Hügel (1778-1803). In 1801 he became Commander of the *Oosthoek* (East Hook) in Surabaya and in 1808 Commander in Batavia. From the marriage to Barbara von Hügel came four children:

1.  Theobald Georg Franz Alexander von Franquemont
2.  Carolina Anna Adriana von Franquemont
3.  Carl Wilhelm von Franquemont, born around 1794, died May 6, 1823, Batavia
4.  Charlotte Elisabeth Piron von Franquemont, born 1797 Ceylon, died 1868 Surabaya, married in first marriage to Mattheis von Franquemont (Batavia 1814) and in second to Hendrik Louis Rademaker (Surabaya 1847). From the marriage to Matteys von Franquemont, among others, the daughter Carolina Josephina von Franquemont (1817-67) was born, who would later become a successful batik entrepreneur.

After the death of his wife, he lived with Henrika Elisabeth Ehrgang. From this union came Hendrik Carl von Franquemont (1806-1838). This son alone had children himself, with Elisabeth van Nuijs (born around 1810 in Surabaya), and passed on the Franquemont name in Java. A son from this marriage was the Carl von Franquemont who was married to the Chinese woman Tan Kiem Nio. This line must likely be regarded as the stem line of most later von Franquemonts in Java and Holland.

Carl David von Franquemont succeeded in placing his son Carl Wilhelm in the administration of the newly forming Dutch administration. Carl Wilhelm was trained as a surgeon and lived as an *assistant surgeon* in 1814 (during the time of the English interregnum) on Bangka and in Batavia. Later he became an official in the Dutch administration. When he died early in 1823, he was one of the so-called 2nd Clerks (*Kommiesen*) in the General Secretariat (third level of the higher administration).

**Philipp August David von Franquemont**, born February 11, 1771, in Stuttgart, died September 11, 1833, in Surabaya. He was a half-brother of Carl David and, like him, also came to Batavia in 1791. There he married the widow C.J. Elbers, born October 6, 1777, in Banyuwangi, died December 30, 1838, in Surabaya. From this marriage came 4 sons and 2 daughters:

1.  Mattheys Johann August David von Franquemont, born December 29, 1795, Surabaya, died October 27, 1844, Surabaya, married to Charlotte Elisabeth von Franquemont (1814 Batavia)
2.  Carel von Franquemont, born 1795, died January 30, 1857, Surabaya, married to Sara Helana Loth (Grisse 1819)
3.  Ludovicus Carolus David (Louis) von Franquemont, born March 20, 1797, Semarang, died April 27, 1862, Surabaya
4.  Wilhelmina von Franquemont, married to Carolus Jacobus Smulders (Batavia 1819)
5.  Everdina Carolina von Franquemont, married to Franciscus Henricus Smulders, Resident of Banten).

Philipp August David von Franquemont applied for naturalization in Batavia in 1813 and acquired a large house on the then-magnificent street Molenvliet. Later we find him in Surabaya, where his son Mattheis had been operating a trading house since 1819 and owned two ships: the *Maria Louisa* (50 t) and *Fathalkair* (31 t). From 1825, the firm was registered under the name of the father, Philipp August David von Franquemont, who had meanwhile relocated to Surabaya. The two other sons, Carel and Heinrich, also lived as merchants in Surabaya at that time. Carel (1795-1857) married Sarah Helena Loth from the neighboring port of Grisse as early as 1819. Mattheis seems to have initially aimed for a military career, for during the English period, on March 12, 1812, an M. von Franquemont was appointed Lieutenant in the *Amboynes Corps*, headquarters in Weltevreden. He was likely not taken over into Dutch service later, or had long since discovered his abilities as a merchant and assessed them as greater.

In the year 1831, the *Almanak voor Nederlandsch Indie* lists three trading houses under the name Franquemont in Surabaya: a) Philipp August David von Franquemont, b) Matthijs von Franquemont, c) Carel von Franquemont and J. de Rooij.

If the ownership of ships says anything about the business success of a merchant in Surabaya, then the Franquemonts' firm reached its peak in 1828. At that time, they owned five merchant ships: the brigs *Harriet, Elisabeth, Margaretha, Gunung Api* and the schooner *Wilhelmina.*

Another brother, Louis von Franquemont, decided on a civil service career and climbed high up the bureaucratic ladder. He entered government service as early as 1811 and was, in 1814, under the English Lieutenant Governor Sir Thomas S. Raffles, an Assistant in the *Secretary's Office* in Buitenzorg. In 1816 he was taken over by the returning Dutch as an administrative official. By 1824 Louis von Franquemont was appointed Head Clerk (*Hoofd Kommies*), a top function in the administration, which he held until 1832; i.e., he sat at an intersection of power during the entire time of the Java War (1825-30). In 1832 Louis was appointed Assistant Resident of Batang, Pekalongan Residency, a position he held until 1836. At the time of the "Cultivation System" (*Kultuurstelsel*), the forced cultivation of agricultural export goods, a position as Assistant Regent or even Regent was very sought-after, for these officials shared in the proceeds of the goods produced in their territory. Only in this way can it be understood that Louis von Franquemont gave up his high civil service post in cultivated Buitenzorg to work in the god-forsaken regency of Batang. In 1836 he took his leave and went into well-earned retirement after 25 years of service: at the age of 39? He subsequently moved to Surabaya to his relatives, where he died in 1862. Shortly before his death, he legitimized his union with the Chinese woman Maria Giok, with whom he had four children. Maria Giok, now Maria von Franquemont, who had been born in 1815, died on April 19, 1880, in Surabaya. Besides the four children with Maria, Louis von Franquemont had five other children with native women. Evidently, he cheerfully continued the tradition of his grandfather Carl Eugen.

But not only the men of the family, but also the women were endowed with a considerable measure of entrepreneurial spirit, and it was to be a woman, the daughter of Mattheis and Charlotte Elisabeth von Franquemont, Carolina Josephina von Franquemont (1817-1867), who—with her batik production for the European/Chinese market, with new designs, new technology, and new marketing—preserved the name Franquemont to the present day.

It was thus this branch of the Württemberg Franquemonts that established itself on Java for the next generations and made a significant contribution to the cultural history of Java.

### Carolina Josephina von Franquemont (1817-1867)

Carolina Josephina von Franquemont was a granddaughter of both Carl David von Franquemont and Philipp August David von Franquemont and thus, in the paternal and maternal line, a great-granddaughter of Duke Carl Eugen of Württemberg.

She belonged, like Constancia Winckelhaagen, née von Mansfeldt (who was to become the first wife of the Javanese painter Raden Saleh), to an extraordinary group of women in the Dutch East Indies whose history has not been sufficiently researched to this day. The women were economically innovative, entrepreneurially active, and thereby wealthy and independent. The core of this group was formed around the middle of the 19th century by young women who appropriated traditional batik production and adapted it to new needs. They managed to make batik sarongs into Indo-European, Indo-Chinese fashion. Thus, after the Java War lost in 1830 had broken the rule of the Javanese princes once and for all, the Dutch claim to power was symbolically transferred to fashion as well. For batik sarongs had previously been reserved for the Javanese aristocracy. With new patterns, new dyeing techniques, and new, mechanically woven cotton fabrics that had a particularly smooth structure, the batik manufacturers of the north coast served a market that was constantly expanding due to the increased immigration of Europeans and the cultural assimilation of Chinese women.

The production of batiks for the European/Chinese market in Java began around 1840 with the participation of "German-Javanese" women. The first and perhaps most successful entrepreneur was Carolina Josephina von Franquemont, who settled in Ungaran near Semarang in 1845. It is possible, even probable, that Constancia Winckelhaagen, Raden Saleh's wife, who came from Semarang and was of German descent like C. J. von Franquemont, got to know and perhaps even learned the art and business of batik making at her place in Ungaran.

Carolina Josephina von Franquemont opened her first batik enterprise in Surabaya in 1840, at the age of 23, which was simultaneously the first batik enterprise of a European woman on Java. She was certainly supported in this by the commercial competence of her father and her brothers. In 1845 she moved production to the (seemingly) healthier Ungaran, a climatic health resort and spa above Semarang. The reason for the move to Ungaran was not only the more pleasant climate but also the greater proximity to a wealthy client base in Semarang (then the second-largest "European" city on the island). Furthermore, the availability of competent batik workers was greater in Central Java than in Surabaya. This circumstance guaranteed the quality of the batik production from the Franquemont workshop.

The secret of Carolina Josephina von Franquemont's business success was the successful color imitation of the coveted Indian Chintz. Besides the usual red, blue, and brown tones that had previously dominated Javanese batiks, she was able to produce a colorfast green. Traditionally, the complementary color green was produced from indigo and turmeric. But since the yellow of the turmeric was not washable, neither was the green. Franquemont's wash-fast grass green became the trademark of her wares. To be sure, it was soon imitated by other manufactories (industrial espionage existed even in those days!), but no one reached the brilliance of the original. Josephina's formula remained unbeaten and she took her secret to the grave.

Carolina Josephina's multi-colored fabrics were so successful because they picked up the taste of rich European and Chinese women and could set new trends alongside it. She drew inspiration from German and Dutch fashion magazines that had made the long sea voyage to Java. According to Rouffaer, Mrs. von Franquemont had secured the exclusive right to market certain patterns from Dutch and German fashion magazines. An entrepreneurial move not to be underestimated.

Besides the color green, she also introduced a fundamentally new spatial layout in the design of batik sarongs. Thus, on the few surviving batiks from her workshop (fewer than 20!), one finds large-scale depictions of fruit trees (nutmeg tree), of fairy tale figures (Little Red Riding Hood), but also depictions of the Java War and Chinese myths. Sometimes she even had Indo-Malay poems written between the figures! She was thus not only a successful businesswoman but also set new standards as a designer that would influence the batik production of the north coast of Java for decades.

On June 10, 1867, the eruption of the volcano Ungaran destroyed her batik manufactory. In the process, Carolina Josephina von Franquemont also lost her life. Raden Saleh's wife, who had lived in Semarang for a long time, certainly knew Carolina Josephina, and it is possible that she aligned the organization of her own batik manufactory in Batavia and its product range with that of Mrs. von Franquemont. Unfortunately, no batiks have been identified to date that originate from the manufactory of Constancia Winckelhaagen, née von Mansfeldt—for that was the full name of Mevrouw Raden Saleh.

Could it be that her husband, the famous painter, assisted her in designing contemporary batik patterns? One must admit that this idea is tempting.

History is not made by people, but by conditions of production, they say. Others say that history waits for the decisive deed of a violent or genial man. If we look at the small history of the illegitimate sons of the Württemberg Duke Carl Eugen, we see to our astonishment what potential and resources the members of these families could activate in cultural foreign lands. But neither the military nor the commercial brilliance of the men has impressed itself upon posterity. It is the art of Carolina Josephina von Franquemont that has allowed the name of von Franquemont to endure into the 21st century.
