Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer (1807 – 1857)

A Dresden Journalist, Daguerreotypist, and Adventurer

**Werner Kraus**

Of all the great technical inventions of the 19th century, photography was the one that spread most rapidly across the world. Only a few years after Louis Daguerre’s first successful attempts to capture light and shadow on a plate and finally on paper, the new processes were en route to the most remote corners of the earth. Hardly a place remained unphotographed. Hardly a people whose bodies and material culture were not depicted, documented, and viewed and evaluated by thousands of European eyes. The European weariness with its own existence, which had gripped the sense of life in broad circles of the old continent in the last quarter of the 19th century, hoped for a certain stimulation from the sight of other worlds. Stimulation of their interest, their vitality, and also their desire.

Shortly after the presentation of the daguerreotype on August 19, 1839, before the members of the French Academy of Sciences, newly trained daguerreotypists swarmed out to capture as many exotic motifs as possible for the astonished public. The most famous result of this first wave of travel photography was the book *Excursions daguerriennes: Vues et monuments les plus remarquables du globe*[^1], compiled by the Parisian optician and instrument maker Noël-Paymal Lerebours. Lerebours collected more than 100 daguerreotypes showing landscapes and monuments from European and Oriental regions. He had these lithographed, thereby making them reproducible. Part of this collection included eight images by Frédérik Goupil-Fesquet, who traveled the eastern Mediterranean, the Levant, and Egypt between 1839 and 1840 with his uncle Horace Vernet, the most famous French painter of that time. His lithographed daguerreotypes juxtaposed the Orientalist paintings of his uncle with a more realistic, or rather complementary, image of the lands and cultures of the Near East. They showed a daily life that had little to do with the romantic fantasies of Orientalist painters. This, however, was mainly due to the technology. A technical image of a landscape or ancient sites simply shows them as they presented themselves to the daguerreotypist. Orientalizing "sweeteners" could not yet be applied to the images back then. That was left to the painters with brush and canvas. However, this does not mean that the daguerreotypist differed in his views on the Orient and its population in any way from those of his painting contemporaries. Frédérik Goupil-Fesquet, the first daguerreotypist of the Near East, supported the *Mission civilisatrice*, the alleged duty of Europeans to "civilize" the cultures of the Near East, just as his painting uncle Horace Vernet did. Goupil-Fesquet writes in memory of the journey with his uncle: “It is pleasant for us to know that under the growing influence of French civilization, the sleeping reason of the country will awaken”[^2]. The new technology thus had no influence on the cultural perception of the Other, just as the new technical gaze entailed no change in Vernet's painting style.[^3]

The publication *Excursions daguerriennes: Vues et monuments les plus remarquables du globe* mainly showed European views. Only eight of the 100 lithographed daguerreotypes showed views from the Near East, originating from Goupil-Fesquet. Not all countries were already "opened" by the European colonial advance and accessible in the same way as the Near East. In the conquest of the world by photography, there were therefore non-simultaneities, though these were not of dramatic proportions.

In South and Southeast Asia—that is, in the established territories of the British East India Company in India and in the colony of the Dutch East Indies, but also in Siam—the new technology was quickly noticed and applied. As early as 1839, a few weeks after the daguerreotype was presented in Paris on August 19, 1839, the Asiatic Society in Kolkata (Calcutta) discussed the new technique, and just one year later a daguerreotype must have been created in that city, namely the image of the new Sans Souci Theatre on Park Street.[^4] This image exists only in lithographed form, and the original daguerreotypist is not known by name. The first commercial daguerreotypist in Kolkata was likely F. M. Montairo, who can be traced from 1844. The next was Friedrich Fiebig from Lower Silesia, who lived in Kolkata from 1846 and created a magnificent photographic panorama of the city there in 1847.[^5]

In the Dutch East Indies as well, the Dutch colony comprising present-day Indonesia, people were convinced early on of the importance of the new imaging process. Possibly, the aforementioned work *Excursions daguerriennes: Vues et monuments les plus remarquables du globe* by Lerebours aroused the desire in the Dutch Ministry of Colonies to see the great Hindu-Javanese structures of Java also taxed as *monuments les plus remarquables du globe*. Good daguerreotyped (and lithographed) images would not only serve science but also show that the Netherlands, alongside looking after their commercial interests, also felt committed to the research and preservation of the local history and culture of Java. The great European narrative that colonial appropriation was to be understood as a benevolent educational program for the colonized peoples—a developmental and educational task generously undertaken—could be beautifully illustrated by the documentation of ancient Javanese monuments.

**Jurriaan Munnich, First Attempts with a Camera in Java**

In November 1841, the Dutch East Indies government equipped Jurriaan Munnich (1816-1865)[^6], a Military Doctor 3rd Class, with a "heliographic camera by Daguerre." With this, he was to take pictures of buildings, landscapes, and antiquities in Java.[^7] Munnich, who had been trained as a *chirurgijn*[^8] at the Rijkshospital in Utrecht, had left the Netherlands on May 12, 1841, aboard the ship "Willem II," which sailed through the Sunda Strait on September 2, 1841,[^9] and arrived shortly thereafter in Batavia.

Why the government entrusted Munnich specifically with this demanding task cannot be determined, as there is no indication that he could demonstrate any experience with the technique of daguerreotypy. Munnich, who was apparently blessed with strong self-confidence (see below), accepted this difficult task without hesitation and failed grandiosely at it. He traveled twice through West and Central Java, and copies of his two reports exist in the National Archives of the Netherlands.[^10] A summarized transcript of the first report appeared in 1842 in the journal *De Kopiist*[^11]. It begins with the following sentences (my translation):

> "Before we describe the newest discovery of photography or photocopying by means of sunlight, we give below an overview of what this art has produced so far. We make use of a report (and have permission to do so) by Mr. J. Munnich, Health Officer 3rd Class with the Indian Medical Service, who is currently undertaking a journey through Java on behalf of the government to take photographic images of the most important sights etc. as well as of plants and other objects of nature. The tools used for this were acquired at the administration's expense, following a recommendation by the fourth class of the Royal Dutch Institute. We add that we wish Mr. Munnich a clear sky on this journey, without which his efforts remain fruitless."

Jurriaan Munnich failed in his attempt to produce daguerreotypes in Java. As mentioned, he was too unfamiliar with the daguerreotype technique, and I assume that in a certain hubris he accepted an assignment he could not fulfill with his limited knowledge. J. Munnich, who lived on Java until the end of 1860 and took positions in various scientific disputes, was known for trusting himself to have expertise in many fields very quickly, including the field of poetry in the Dutch East Indies. As early as 1842, only a few months after his arrival in the colony, he published an essay titled "*Iets omtret de beoefening der Poezij in Neerlands Indie*" (Something about the Practice of Poetry in the Dutch East Indies)[^12]. This hasty contribution was mercilessly torn apart by two authors in the very next issues of the same journal.[^13] J. Munnich, Health Officer 3rd Class, thus did not have a good start in the intellectual life of Java, and the failure of his photographic assignment would not have supported his reputation initially either. Incidentally, there is not a single reference to Munnich’s failed daguerreotype attempts in the newspapers of Java at that time.[^14] Obviously, Munnich succeeded in keeping his failure out of the public discourse for the time being. And obviously, the administration did not oblige him to settle the costs incurred.

It seems, and I draw this conclusion from a newspaper article cited below[^15], that Munnich did not perceive his failure as personal, but blamed certain peculiarities of the tropical climate, namely "solar heat and rising vapors," which would prevent an accurate and sharp depiction of objects and persons. In doing so, he (or the author of the newspaper article) relied on the miasma theory common in medicine at the time. Miasma, the Greek term for pollution, was understood as an exhalation from the ground arising especially under strong sunlight, through putrefaction and decomposition, in damp or swampy places. These miasmas, according to the historical theory, rise, contaminate the air, and are spread by the wind.[^16] A remnant of this long-outdated medical theory, incidentally, is the term "malaria," which is composed of the terms *mala* (bad) and *aria* (air).

The newspaper report on daguerreotypy that appeared in Batavia on February 22, 1845, first presents the arguments that Munnich might have held responsible for his failure. It states (my translation):

> "Not too long ago, the idea was prevalent here that Mr. Daguerre's invention, namely to achieve the depiction of objects through the imprint of light, would probably not be able to bear fruit for the tropical regions of Java. It was assumed that the constant movement in the atmosphere, caused by solar heat and rising vapors, was an insurmountable obstacle for the sharp representation of objects. Various attempts showed no favorable results. Although certain images appeared on the metal plates, they were hardly satisfactory. Everything was indistinct, and there could be no talk of an accurate reproduction of forms. No faithful copies of nature were created."[^17]

The newspaper report, however, did not leave it at this negative assessment of the possibilities of daguerreotypy in Java, but continued:

> "One had already resigned oneself to the fact that Mr. Daguerre's art could not achieve the same successes here in Java as in Europe. But then came Mr. Schäfer in June of the past year (1844) and the uncertainty came to an end. Shortly after his arrival, Mr. Schäfer found that the local atmosphere was no obstacle whatsoever, but even an advantage for the production of daguerreotypes. His first attempts, taken in Buitenzorg, confirmed his assumption, and since then he has delivered so many and such beautiful results that his opinion must be considered confirmed."[^18]

Mr. Schäfer must therefore not only be described as the first successful daguerreotypist of Java but also as the one who thoroughly did away with the miasma theory in photography. He was, so to speak, the Robert Koch of tropical photography. Schäfer left behind a large number of daguerreotypes which, happily and astonishingly, have survived to this day.

**Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer**

Who was Mr. Schäfer? The daguerreotypist Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer (1807-1857) was known only to specialists until a few years ago. In the 1980s, the Dutch photo historian Dr. Herman Jan Moeshart described Adolph Schäfer as the first professional photographer in the Dutch East Indies and contextualized the significance of his surviving daguerreotypes. However, these Javanese works were not the beginning of Schäfer's career as a daguerreotypist, for this began back in Dresden and then continued in The Hague. These beginnings are unfortunately still unexplored, and we do not yet know any early results by Schäfer.

Schäfer became known in photographic literature not through his early works in Dresden or The Hague, but through his daguerreotypes produced in the Dutch East Indies colony. These metal plates, which are kept today in the Print Room of the Leiden University Library, are the oldest photographic documents we know from Java and at the same time the only daguerreotypes by Schäfer that survived the times. The small insight that Moeshart opened up for us into the life of Adolph Schäfer is based solely on a bundle of letters and official documents that Moeshart found and analyzed in the Nationaal Archief in The Hague. It covers only 3 years of Adolph Schäfer's life and demonstrates how important and at the same time limited colonial archival materials are. For the colonial administration, Schäfer was interesting only as a cost factor. When this ceased, his life in the files also came to an end. Without supplements from other sources, archival pieces are often misleading. This also applies to the present case. If we were to content ourselves solely with the material Moeshart found in The Hague, Adolph Schäfer would appear to us as a rather one-dimensional man, as a photographer who traveled to Java in the service of the Dutch Ministry of Colonies only to fail there in the documentation of archaeological sites.

This essay is an attempt to bring Adolph Schäfer out of this one-dimensionality and to reconstruct a life that spans from birth to death. The result astonished the writer himself. I never would have guessed what a rich and complex life this man led.

Until now, we knew little about Schäfer's life before his departure for Java—when and where was he born, where did he live, and what work did he pursue before the invention of the daguerreotype? And we knew just as little about the 12 years between the end of his photographic contract with the colonial government (1845) and his death in Batavia on January 3, 1857.[^19] For a long time, experts were also unclear whether Schäfer succeeded in returning to Europe.

This contribution attempts to shed light on the two unknown periods of Adolph Schäfer's life: his time in Dresden until 1843 and his time in Java between 1845 and 1857. Some of these years are well documented and can be deciphered; others remain hidden in the dim realm of possibilities and can only be presented speculatively.

But before I turn to the hitherto unknown, I would like to first describe those few years that Schäfer spent in the service of the colonial authorities in The Hague and Batavia, which were to become decisive for his fate. In doing so, I rely on the research results of Herrman J. Moeshart, which he published in a series of articles. The most comprehensive essay is his contribution to the exhibition catalog "Silber und Salz" (Silver and Salt)[^20], in which Moeshart not only quotes extensively from a memorandum by Schäfer but also shows a large part of his surviving daguerreotypes. At the same time, I can contribute some details about Schäfer's later years in Java, about the time not covered by Moeshart. In the end, however, I would like to return to the beginning and describe Schäfer's work in Dresden between the years 1835 and 1843. Particularly through the consideration of his Dresden years, the image Moeshart constructed of Adolph Schäfer will change significantly. We will meet a man who fought uprightly and on the front lines for a liberal Germany and who saw his future and the future of his family in an emigrant colony in America and not in Java. His fatal decision to accept an offer from the Dutch government and embark on the adventurous journey to Java was to change his life from the ground up and destroy it. The planned short professional stay in Java turned out to be a trap from which he tragically could not escape.

**Schäfer in Java**

After the daguerreotype attempts of the military doctor Jurriaan Munnich had failed miserably, the Colonial Ministry started a second attempt in 1844 to introduce photography to the Dutch East Indies. This time, an experienced photographer was to be sent to Java, one who demonstrably mastered his craft from the ground up and who could present evidence of this to the Ministry. The choice fell on Adolph Schäfer, who had been offering his services as a photographer in The Hague since January 1, 1843, and was apparently achieving good success.[^21]

[Image: Schäfer's advertisement in *Dagblad van 's Gravenhage*, 02-01-1843]

A little later, Schäfer announced in another advertisement that he could only offer his services for a few more days, as he would leave the city on February 14, 1843.[^22]

In choosing Schäfer, the Ministry relied not only on the daguerreotypes he submitted but also on a recommendation from the Würzburg physician and Japan researcher Philipp Franz Baltasar von Siebold. Siebold had acquired an excellent scientific reputation in the Netherlands through his botanical works on Japanese flora and also maintained a good relationship with the Dutch King Willem II. Schäfer evidently visited Siebold and asked him for support for his planned trip to Java.[^23] Why Schäfer, who actually wanted to travel to England, Ireland, and possibly the United States of America, changed his plans in The Hague and suddenly had Java in mind cannot be reconstructed through any document. But in The Hague, of course, everyone spoke of the colony which, through the *cultuurstelsel* (Cultivation System)—the forced cultivation of agricultural export goods introduced 10 years earlier—was developing into a goldmine for the Netherlands. And naturally, many adventurous or desperate people believed that some yellow dust would stick to them during a walk through the goldmine. For some, this hope would be fulfilled, but for many others, the passage to the promised colony became a journey into failure and personal destruction. Schäfer ultimately joined the ranks of the failures, but no one could have guessed that in the spring of 1843.

Philipp Franz Baltasar von Siebold was enthusiastic about the new imaging technique, the daguerreotype, and convinced that the photographic depiction of the colony had scientific value. Perhaps he also hoped for a better representation of Java's plant world than could be achieved by the few draftsmen in the colony. At the same time, Siebold was also impressed by Adolph Schäfer's expertise, for in his letter of recommendation to the King, he expressed himself very positively about Schäfer's photographic works. He wrote:

> "Mr. Schäfer occupies himself with the production of daguerreotypes and has made such progress in this field of craftsmanship that he may be counted among the most skilled practitioners of this art."[^24]

Whether Siebold arrived at this judgment solely through viewing Schäfer's daguerreotypes from The Hague may be doubted. Perhaps Adolph Schäfer presented his "press kit" to him, or showed him a notice that had appeared in the liberal Dresden newspaper *Sächsische Vaterlandsblätter* on January 8, 1843. It stated:

> "The former publisher and editor of these papers, Adolph Schäfer, has left Saxony to first make a journey to Holland, England, Scotland, and Ireland, where he intends to daguerreotype. He practices this important invention of modern times with a perfection such as we have not yet known in Germany and even in France; the most judicious men, e.g., Prof. Moser in Königsberg, declared: that Schäfer's pictures are the most beautiful that have been produced to date. May the practice of the art grant him compensation for so much vexation that is inseparable from the work of the journalist."[^25]

King Willem II was initially hesitant and did not answer Schäfer's petition of May 6, 1843. Only when the Minister of Colonies Jean Chretien Baud pressed the King for an answer on June 14, 1843, did the latter agree to Schäfer's petition on June 17, 1843. Schäfer was to receive free passage to Java, and at the same time, he was granted a generous loan to procure the best technical equipment. Baud assumed, by the way, that Schäfer wanted to set up a studio for daguerreotypy in Batavia.[^26] The Minister was a great patron of the Javanese painter Raden Saleh, who was living in Dresden at that time, and it is to be assumed that he inquired with Schäfer about his standing in Dresden.[^27]

The approval for free passage to Batavia included the condition that Schäfer should be available to the colonial administration in Java to photographically support the scientific work of the government archaeologist W.A. van den Ham at the Buddhist stupa Borobudur in the Kedu Residency (today Central Java).

Before Schäfer embarked for Java, however, he first traveled (with the blessing of the Dutch government) to Paris to equip himself with the latest daguerreotype devices. He bought two cameras and a quantity of spare parts from the optician Charles Chevalier, whom he regarded as the best manufacturer of photographic equipment. Furthermore, he purchased accessories and a stock of 750 plates of various sizes. The entire equipment, plus chemicals, cost the enormous sum of 8882.00 Francs (4250 Dutch Guilders). Although the government paid in advance, it charged this amount to Schäfer as a debt. Thus the foundation for Schäfer's later misery in Java was laid, for he would never succeed in servicing or reducing the constantly accumulating mountain of debt.

In Paris, Schäfer used the time to perfect his art. He visited all the larger studios and a number of the well-known photographers of his time. Among others, he met for a professional discussion with the inventor of the daguerreotype, L.J.M. Daguerre.[^28] On the return journey from Paris to Holland, at the end of 1843, Schäfer traveled via Dresden to say goodbye to his family. He would not see them again.[^29]

On February 4, 1844, Adolph Schäfer left Holland on the sailing ship *Diligentia* and reached Batavia on June 1, 1844. Since the colonial government had no clear use for Schäfer yet, he opened a studio for daguerreotypy on September 16 in the premises of the *Utrechtsche Apotheek* in Rijswijk in Batavia. The owner of the Utrecht Pharmacy, F. Göring[^30], was a German pharmacist and botanist. It may have been easier for Schäfer to shelter with a compatriot.

[Image: Javasche Courant, 7 September 1844]

Later he moved a few streets further to Gang Chaulan (today Jalan Hasyim Ashari, near Jl. Gajah Mada). A large report in the newspaper *Javasche Courant* of February 22, 1845, describes Schäfer's work in Batavia in the most positive colors and also reports on the large number of very successful portrait daguerreotypes.[^31] However, he soon had to give up his flourishing business. On February 15, 1845, he announced to the public that he felt compelled to close his studio.[^32]

At this time, Schäfer had been obliged by the government to daguerreotype a part of the Hindu-Javanese sculptures of the Museum of the *Bataviaasche Genootschap voor Kunsten en Wetenschapen*. This was intended as a way to force him to pay back part of the money advanced by the government. In addition to the sculptures, Schäfer was to record the recently purchased Wayang puppets by the Genootschap, which represented the characters of the Balinese shadow play. It was planned to have these daguerreotypes lithographed later and included as illustrations in the museum's catalog.[^33] In the negotiations, Schäfer tried to set the prices for his daguerreotypes as high as possible. It was obviously his goal to become debt-free in the foreseeable future in order to save a certain financial cushion for his return to Europe. This, however, he did not succeed in doing. Instead of the 120-150 Guilders demanded per image, the *Genootschap* paid only 800 Guilders for the entire assignment. Since this included 66 images of objects and some additional daguerreotypes, Schäfer received not even 10% of the sum he demanded and less for the individual image than he could have demanded in Holland.[^34] This failed deal made it clear that he possessed no bargaining power in Java whatsoever, but was entirely dependent on the goodwill of the government—a goodwill that, however, did not exist. Just 7 months after his arrival in Batavia, the beginning of his downfall was thus outlined.

In April 1845, he was asked to take up his work at the *Genootschap voor Kunsten en Wetenschapen* "without further delay." Although he did not agree with the conditions, he could not evade this demand. His daguerreotypes made in the museum, which have largely survived, are brilliant proof of his competence. He succeeded in producing unusual depths of field, and the way he arranged the sculptures also revealed his special skill.

After Schäfer had completed the work in the museum, he proceeded to Central Java to dedicate himself to the main reason for his journey, the documentation of archaeological evidence. He left Batavia in September 1845 and traveled along the Great Post Road by carriage to Semarang and from there over the mountains to the old royal city of Surakarta. His monthly advance had been increased to 600 Guilders for the duration of the trip, and the use of post horses was free. The journey went through some of the most spectacular landscapes of Java, such as the ascent of the Megamendung Pass from Bogor to Cianjur, and Schäfer must have had a strong urge here and there to unpack his camera and take pictures of the variety of tropical nature. Whether he had the opportunity to do so, we do not know. In any case, no recordings of this kind have survived. At the end of 1845, the Dutch Resident of the Kedu Regency/Central Java reported to the Governor-General in Batavia that Schäfer had delivered to him 58 daguerreotypes, mounted in 13 glass frames. Attached to this report was a memorandum by Schäfer in which he described the difficult conditions under which he had to work at Borobudur. No stone building was available to him there, but only a wind- and dust-permeable bamboo hut, which moreover could not really be darkened. Because of these deficiencies, he finally had to stop the work. Photographing the friezes in the lower galleries of Borobudur also caused great problems. The galleries are so narrow that the cameras were unable to establish the distance necessary for depth of field. Schäfer was not prepared to continue working under these adverse conditions and was asked to discuss the pending problems with the archaeologist W. H. van den Ham[^35] in Surakarta. The latter thought that it made little sense to photograph individual isolated reliefs, as the depicted story could not be worked out from them. Only the reconstruction of the depicted story would allow conclusions about the Buddhist school of the builders of Borobudur and thus about the dating of the largest Buddhist structure in the world. According to van den Ham's idea, each relief should be photographed individually and in the correct order. This would have required the enormous number of 5000 to 6000 plates and several years of work. The government refused to accept the expected large expenses. Unfortunately, the archaeologist W. H. van den Ham died of fever at just that time, and the government in Batavia took this misfortune as an occasion to end the Borobudur project.

Since September 30, 1845, Jan Jacob Rochussen had been in office as the new Governor-General in Batavia. Jan Jacob Rochussen, formerly a successful Finance Minister of the Netherlands, was known as a liberal politician during his time as a minister in Holland. But in the colony, he increasingly adopted a conservative and authoritarian stance. He had no great interest in art or archaeology, and the documentation of the reliefs of Borobudur was solely a waste of money for Rochussen. He wrote in a report to the Ministry in The Hague about Schäfer:

> "... his high demands and few good reasons to give Mr. Schäfer a position as a daguerreotypist; furthermore, the possibility nullified by the death of Mr. van den Ham to use Mr. Schäfer to produce photographs of the most important archaeological objects found in Java, lead to the decision to refrain from producing further images of bas-reliefs of the temple of Borobudur."[^36]

Schäfer was relieved of his duties and confronted with the gigantic mountain of debt of 24,240 Guilders and 90 Cents. This debt was composed of the accumulated advances the government had granted Schäfer and the acquisition costs of the equipment. Why the government went into this large financial advance payment and why they believed that a daguerreotypist in the colony could generate such a sum will remain a mystery.

Schäfer's monthly income was stopped. He was only guaranteed free travel to Semarang, the city of importance located closest to Borobudur. There he was to settle as a daguerreotypist and assign one-third of his income to the government as repayment of his debts. It is not difficult to imagine that Schäfer was deeply affected by the negative development of his career and that he probably fell into a state of depression. He had a wife and children waiting for him in Dresden, whom he hoped to support generously through his trip to Java. Instead, he now sat in Semarang and was supposed to pay off his astronomical debts to the colonial government with portrait photographs. Obviously, he was unable to establish a flourishing studio in Semarang. In June 1848, the Resident of Semarang reported to the government in Batavia that

> "... the income of Schaefer was so low that he was generally considered someone who possessed little or no money and whose performance had declined. There is no purpose for him to remain here anymore, and he himself has no more prospects of paying off his debts in this country. Therefore, he should be allowed a trip to Surabaya, Madura, and Sumanap, so that he can check whether daguerreotypes can be sold there."[^37]

Surabaya in East Java was the most important industrial and trading city in Java. The inhabitants of the city were more open to modernity than the officials and merchants in Batavia and therefore offered a certain market for the new technique of daguerreotypy. The situation was similar with the small principalities on the island of Madura adjacent to Surabaya. In both Bangkalan and Sumenep, the princes lived on a good state pension, which they earned by renting out their troops to the colonial power. And both courts were ruled by enlightened princes open to technical innovations. As markets, however, the small principalities could only play a minor role, as the number of people to be portrayed was quite limited. Whether and when Adolph Schäfer practiced in Surabaya and Madura is not known. There are no documents about it. In the newspapers of Surabaya from that time, no advertisement can be found announcing Schäfer as a daguerreotypist.[^38] The fact is that the government extended his permit for Surabaya by one year and additionally granted him permission to travel to Rembang and Jepara, two cities in East Java. When this too changed nothing about his income situation, the authorities ordered him back to Batavia in August 1849 to finally organize the settlement of his debts. When it was clear that he could not repay his accumulated debt mountain, the government confiscated his cameras and had them auctioned. The ridiculous proceeds of 227.11 Guilders went to the state treasury. For the daguerreotypes delivered by him, 5679 Guilders were invoiced; the remaining debt of 17,480.36 Guilders had to be written off.

Without his photographic equipment, Schäfer was now doubly unable to earn sufficient money, and it is not clear how he earned his livelihood. It is not recorded that he was able to procure a new camera. But Schäfer seems to have believed, even in this precarious situation, that he would soon return to Europe. He probably assumed that the Dutch government, which had financed his free passage to Java, would now also ensure that he could travel back to Holland just as freely. In any case, the magazine "Leipziger Charivari" reported on March 18, 1850:

> "Adolph Schäfer from Leipzig, son of the well-known philologist, founder of the Vaterländische Blätter, who went to the Indies about six years ago as an attaché of the Dutch government, is on his way home, in possession of a rich travel journal, a quantity of daguerreotyped landscapes, a not insignificant collection of value for the natural sciences." [^39]

Unfortunately, the announced journey home could not be realized, as he was refused free passage and he could not raise the funds to finance it himself. Thus, as far as I know, neither his *rich travel journal* nor his *daguerreotyped landscapes* came to Europe. Both would be of inestimable importance to us today.

After the dramatic failure of his plans, Adolph Schäfer withdrew in disappointment to Yogyakarta. He probably had acquaintances there on whose support he hoped. in Yogya he lived, according to colonial custom, with a Javanese woman, a Nyai, with whom he fathered a child. In the baptismal register of the city of Semarang from the year 1852, the entry is found: *Fredrich Adolph Hendrik, born in Djokjakarta, on September 16, 1851, baptized in Semarang on May 27, 1852; Parents: Philip (sic!) Adolph Heinrich Schaefer and the Javanese woman Sarina, unmarried people.*[^40]

Apparently, Schäfer had informed his wife Fanny in Dresden about the unhappy development in Java and also written to her about his fatherhood. For Fanny Schäfer demanded a divorce in 1851.[^41] Through the baptism in Semarang, Schäfer recognized and legitimized his son as his lawful child. It is not known what became of this son. He was only 6 years old at his father's death.

Until now, it was assumed that the first photographer active in Yogyakarta was the Swede Cesar von Düben. In January 1858, he produced a series of portraits of Hamangkubuwono VI and other members of the Sultan's family. A little later, in 1859, Samuel Willem Camerik (1830-1897) settled in the city. Camerik was born on Bangka as the son of a Dutch doctor and a Chinese woman. After the death of his mother, he moved with his depressive father to Holland, where, as far as can be determined, he received no real training. At the end of the 1850s, he went back to Java alone at first and worked as a drawing teacher at the Pupil Corps in Gombang (a military institution for Indo-European children). Since his salary there was so low that he saw no possibility of accumulating enough money to have his wife follow him from Holland, he resigned from the service in 1859 and settled in Yogyakarta—initially as a draftsman and portrait painter, but soon as a photographer. Camerik was quickly successful and was soon able to bring his wife from Holland to Yogyakarta. He even rose to become the court photographer of the Sultan of Yogyakarta and became the teacher of the first Javanese photographer, Kassian Cephas (1845-1912).[^42]

Since Schäfer's stay in Yogyakarta is proven by the baptismal notice of his son, one must acknowledge that neither von Düben nor Camerik, but Schäfer was the first professional photographer in the city. Unfortunately, no works by Schäfer from that time have been proven so far.

After the baptism of his son in May 1852 in Semarang, the young family seems to have moved to Batavia. In the *Almanak voor Nederlandsch Indie* of the years 1853 to 1856, Schäfer appears as a resident of the city (the Almanak reports the names of residents always one year later—Almanak 1853 contains the names of 1852). Incidentally, in the years between 1845 and 1852, he cannot be tracked down in any address directory. As a nomadic photographer, he seems to have possessed no right of settlement anywhere. Two documents from the year 1853 (they are the last before his death on January 3, 1857) indicate that he lived in Batavia in the street Sawa Bezar (today *Jl. Sukarjo Wiryopranoto*, Jakarta)—not far from his first apartment in Gang Chaulan. Truly not a lordly area.

On May 4, 1853, Schäfer announced in the newspaper *Java-bode* that books could be submitted to him for auctions and that he was willing to create catalogs for libraries.[^43]

[Image: Java-bode, 4 May 1853.]

A month later, another advertisement by A. Schäfer is found in the same newspaper. This time he offered an unusual "*Solair-Mikroskoop [Solar Microscope], manufactured by Ch. Chevalier in Paris*", for sale.[^44]

[Image: Java-bode, 4 June 1853]

It was probably the last of his devices bought in Paris, his last memory of a better time and at the same time the last trace he left in the Dutch East Indies. Adolph Schäfer passed away on January 3, 1857, unnoticed by the public, in Batavia. No death notice in any newspaper and no grave on any of the European cemeteries of the city. He was probably laid in a pauper's grave by his Javanese wife.

Only in the *Javasche courant* of August 22, 1857, did the name Adolph Schäfer appear once more. There, the *Weeskamer* in Batavia, the office for orphans, asked the question whether there were still any people in Java to whom Adolph Schäfer owed money, or who owed him money. Probably his small son was admitted to an orphanage in the city at this time, and the authorities wanted to ensure that all financial claims were settled.

Thus ended the life of the daguerreotypist Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer in 1857 in misery in Batavia. The great plans that had carried him to Java had failed cruelly. That Schäfer was a talented photographer is proven by the surviving daguerreotypes. But he had no chance to defend himself against the capricious decisions of the government in Batavia. When the newly arrived Governor-General Rochussen believed that Schäfer was no longer needed, he let him fall into the abyss. Schäfer was neither able to pay his debts nor could he save up the enormous costs for the return trip to Europe, especially not after the administration had confiscated and auctioned his entire equipment. Now he no longer had any possibility to work in his ancestral profession. How poorly he tried to get by is shown by the advertisement of May 4, 1853, in which he offered his services as an auctioneer of books. But the business of auctioneers, which was an important one in Batavia, had long been in the hands of large business houses, and Schäfer will hardly have had a chance to act successfully in this field. His last attempt to gain an economic foothold was just as doomed to failure as all previous ones.

**Schäfer's Origins, Education, and Training in Saxony**

Now that we have first dealt with Schäfer's time in Java and tied together known and previously unknown facts, I would like to describe his life before his departure, which was previously a single blank spot in research. And this although the first 35 years in the life of Adolph Schäfer were extraordinarily successful.

Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer was born on January 11, 1807, in Leipzig.[^45] He was a son of the philologist Gottfried Heinrich Schäfer (1764-1840)[^46], a restless, easily offended book scholar who taught at the University of Leipzig for some time. In 1817, Gottfried Heinrich Schäfer sold his valuable specialized philological library comprising 6500 volumes for the astonishing amount of 10,000 Thalers[^47] to the Saxon King Friedrich August I (1750-1827). The collection was incorporated into the library of the University of Leipzig, and its former owner was simultaneously appointed the second librarian of the University of Leipzig. Gottfried Heinrich Schäfer, Adolph Schäfer's father, thus belonged to the intellectual inventory of Leipzig and, due to the sale of his library, was also to be regarded as wealthy.

His son Friedrich Heinrich Adolph studied law in Leipzig from 1827 and completed these studies in 1833. For that time, 6 years was an astonishingly long study period, and it may be that he had to interrupt his studies. A Wikipedia entry on Adolph Schäfer claims that he first completed an apprenticeship as a hatter, but there is no proof for this and it appears to me as absolutely incredible. Since the mentioned entry on Wikipedia shows a number of errors, it is advisable to also view the hatter episode with caution. After completing his studies in Leipzig, Schäfer moved to Dresden. He had been accepted into the Saxon civil service, specifically into the Ministry of Cultus, and his place of duty was the royal seat of Dresden. There he is traceable in the address book from 1833.[^48]

In 1833, Schäfer was 26 years old and lived in the Schlossgasse in Dresden's Old Town. His status is given in the address book as Bacc. Jur., i.e., lawyer with completed training. In 1834 and 1835 he is registered in the Pirnaer Gasse, while he is not mentioned in the Dresden resident register in 1836. Perhaps he had been temporarily transferred or was completing his education with a dissertation. On August 1, 1837, he received his certificate of appointment as Secretary at the Ministry for Cultus and Instruction.[^49] Thus Schäfer had succeeded within a few years in a considerable ascent in the Saxon bureaucracy. He now appears in the address books as "Ministerial Secretary" until the year 1839.

The Kingdom of Saxony, which had allied itself early with Napoleon, had to accept large territorial losses at the Congress of Vienna (1815) and thus lost a substantial part of its political significance. Culturally, however, things looked quite different. Dresden had experienced another peak of its brilliant cultural history during the Romantic period. Poets and painters flocked to the city, and the Dresden Picture Gallery ensured that the city became a cultural center of Europe, adorned by its visitors with the proud name "Florence on the Elbe." But the intellectual life of Dresden also attracted a large number of rentiers, wealthy citizens who enjoyed their retirement in the mild climate of the city and ensured that the opera and theater could be staffed with outstanding forces. Politically, however, Dresden was and remained a stronghold of aristocratic conservatism, and the two historical attempts to equip the country with a more liberal constitution (1831 and 1848/49) succeeded only to a limited extent. The tough resistance of the royal court, but also the stolid resistance of the conservative bourgeoisie against deeper liberal reforms, did not allow the liberalization of the country longed for by many. Most Dresdeners were not bothered by this. They settled comfortably and in Biedermeier fashion into the public space defined by strict political boundaries. Other few, who did not want to accept this, had to leave the city sooner or later. Richard Wagner, Gottfried Semper, Robert and Clara Schumann, and many others went this way. Adolph Schäfer was also one of them.

Schäfer seems to have chafed considerably against the rigid political and social corset of the city over the years. On September 7, 1839, he took the unusual step of submitting his resignation from office. somewhat cryptically, he mentions in his petition that he must take this step *"due to circumstances which I am compelled to take into account"*[^50]. How this decision came about is not transmitted. Very few civil servants of that time voluntarily renounced the privileges accorded to them by civil servant status. After all, a ministerial official earned 500 Thalers a year, had prospects of regular promotion and a state pension. Furthermore, Schäfer was already married at this time and had a family to support. Why then did he carry out this unusual step?

I assume that his decision was a political one. The politically conscious Adolph Schäfer was no longer able to serve the repressive system of the Saxon government. Not even the School and Culture Ministry of Hans Georg von Carlowitz (1772-1840). Minister Carlowitz, who had vehemently spoken out against the French Revolution, was nevertheless a liberal man whose life was characterized by Pietist piety and charity. Nevertheless, in his office as Minister of Cultus, he fulfilled the will of the arch-conservative King Friedrich August II. Carlowitz too ensured that in Saxony the trees of freedom might not grow into the sky.

Adolph Schäfer's political basic attitude was that of a liberal citizen who, like many intellectuals of his time, advocated for a united, republican (or at least for a constitutional-monarchical) Germany. The tension between his personal political views and his official activity obviously became so great that Schäfer did not want to endure it any longer.

Perhaps there was also an insult in office that prompted him to withdraw in offense. After all, this behavioral pattern was well known to him from his childhood, for his father was a town-known social phobic who was in dispute with all sorts of persons and institutions throughout his life.

But perhaps he simply had other plans for his still young life. In any case, Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer decided in 1839 to hang up his civil service career to found a liberal newspaper, the *Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen*, and to lead it editorially.

Schäfer's profession was given in the Dresden address book from 1839 as "Advocate." He lived first in the Schulgasse and moved via the Marienstraße finally into the renowned Rosmarin Gasse. This last move may have happened in connection with his marriage. On March 12, 1840, Schäfer's father died, and the son wrote the following obituary for his father in the Sächsische Vaterlandsblätter on March 14, 1840:

> *On the 12th of this month, in the sixth morning hour, Professor Gottfried Heinrich Schäfer of Leipzig fell asleep gently, after a short sickbed, in his 77th year of life: a true Christian, a faithful, best friend to his own; as a scholar, an ornament of the Fatherland.*
>
> *Adolph Schäfer.*[^51]

That his father, Gottfried Heinrich Schäfer, was apparently known far beyond the borders of Saxony is proven by the notice of his passing in the newspaper *Journal de la Haye* appearing in The Hague[^52]

[Image: Journal de La Haye]

Some time before the death of his father, Adolph Schäfer had entered into the state of matrimony. The already mentioned Wikipedia entry claims that he married *"Franziska Josepha Lövy from Agram (Zagreb)"* *"on September 15, 1838"* in *"Wittgenstein near Chemnitz"*. Now, there is no place called Wittgenstein near Chemnitz, but only a Wittgensdorf. Also, his wife is never called "Franziska Josepha" in later documents, but only "Fanny." I therefore assume that Schäfer did not marry Franziska Josepha Lövy, but Fanny Hofer.

**Fanny Hofer-Schäfer**

Fanny Hofer was a promising young singer at the Court Opera Theater (Kärnthnerthortheater) in Vienna at the beginning of the 1830s. The Vienna journal "Der Sammler" reported about her:

> "A surprising appearance of a different kind was Dlle. Hofer (a student of Mad. Gottdank), who previously belonging to the choir, took a bold swing and debuted as the White Lady. A beautiful figure recommended her first to the eyes; a beautiful voice, quite a bit of certainty and coloratura to the ear of those present."[^53]

After she had successfully made her debut in Vienna, she was a well-known singer for some time, for whom other houses of the k.u.k. monarchy also strove. Thus she was engaged at the opera in Pressburg (Bratislava)[^54] in the winter of 1830/31 and in Brno in the winter of 1833/34.[^55] Afterwards she moved on to Lviv, where however her star already began to sink. On October 1, 1834, the press reported that Fanny Hofer had left the ensemble of the Lviv Opera.[^56] After that, her trace is lost in the press for the time being, so that we cannot reconstruct where Fanny Hofer stayed before her marriage to Adolph Hofer [sic - likely means Schäfer], which perhaps took place in September 1838. Even after the marriage to Schäfer, she held on to her old identity and integrated her name into her new existence. She now called herself Dr. Fanny Schäfer-Hofer and gave birth to 3 children between 1839 and 1842.[^57]

When Adolph Schäfer was no longer able to support her through transfers from the Dutch East Indies, she moved to Leipzig in August 1849 to work there as an esteemed singing teacher. In 1851 she initiated the divorce and was simultaneously appointed as the successor to the famous singer and music pedagogue Henriette Bünau-Grabau (1805-1852)[^58] at the Leipzig Conservatory founded by Mendelssohn-Bartholdy. But already in 1853 she left and founded her own training center for singing and acting in Leipzig.[^59] She left this in 1854 to her colleague Ludwig Schäfer (who was no relative). In 1855, Mrs. Dr. Fanny Schäfer-Hofer (as she was called in the press) married the tenor Claus and moved with him to Freiburg im Breisgau.

**Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen**

After his departure from the Saxon civil service, Schäfer founded the moderate-liberal newspaper "*Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen*" (Dresden Weekly for Patriotic Interests) in October 1839, which appeared in the publishing house of the court bookseller Traugott Bromme. Owner and concession holder was Schäfer himself. In the first issue, the editor explained that the newspaper was to be the successor to the journal "*Der Sammler aller Merkwürdigkeiten, in Beziehung auf Geschichte, Alterthum, Kunst, Natur und Gewerbe in Königreich Sachsen*". However, the new paper would differ from the predecessor in that it was to be "*a paper of the opposition in the noblest sense of the word*"[^60]

[Image]

The high ethical approach that Schäfer pursued in his newspaper is found in a philosophical contribution on the connection between nationality and cosmopolitanism. Schäfer comes to the following statement:

> "*But just as every individual life is determined to be a harmonious one, so too the life of peoples; climate, location, historical memories and language call forth separations, and thereby nationality; but there is indeed on a higher stage of development also something that unites again, and on its last produces cosmopolitanism.*"[^61]

Here Schäfer stands clearly in the intellectual tradition of Immanuel Kant and Christoph Wieland, who believed in the inseparable unity of humanity.

But the scientific and artistic developments of that time also quickly find entry into the *Vaterlandsblätter*. Already in the second number, published on October 12, 1839, an article on daguerreotypy was printed on p.16.[^62] Under the headline "The Daguerreotype," a description of the technique of the new light art is found. The article was, however, not a result of Schäfer's thinking, but processed a series of information that he had found in other sources.[^63]

Schäfer's actual passion, however, was political writing and, as a trained lawyer, he concentrated mainly on constitutional questions.[^64]

Unfortunately, the "*Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen*" found no large readership within the conservative Dresden bourgeoisie. Neither did other intellectuals offer their collaboration, nor did the number of subscribers reach a volume that could have secured the economic existence of the paper. Schäfer therefore looked for comrades-in-arms and co-financiers. He found these in the circle of the radical-democratic publicist Robert Blum.

From November 3, 1840, the *Dresdener Wochenblatt für vaterländischen Interessen* appeared under the new name "*Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter*". Adolph Schäfer is initially named again as owner and editor-in-chief, but the influence of Robert Blum, one of the leading politicians of the Vormärz movement in Germany, was clearly palpable. Blum ensured that the *Vaterlands-Blätter* became politicized and was soon reviled by Metternich's spies with the label "ultra-liberal." By the summer of the year 1841, Blum and his circle took over the opinion leadership of the paper and built it into the most important organ of Young Germany. It was agreed that Schäfer should initially remain formally editor (he held the concession), but the management was taken over by Robert Friese, a close friend of Robert Blum.[^65] Although Schäfer had already asked the Ministry to hand over the editorship of the paper to "the laudably known man of letters, theater secretary Robert Blum"[^66], this was rejected by the censorship authority. Robert Blum could not replace Schäfer in office because the authorities consistently refused him the concession of a newspaper publisher. When Schäfer left the newspaper at the end of 1842, Blum's brother-in-law, Georg Günther, therefore stepped in and took over the editorship of the paper. Upon Schäfer's departure, a mocking poem was sent to him anonymously, which he, the mocked editor, had published in a grand gesture. It read:

*He zealously served the Fatherland,*

*Scolded and quarreled for his daily bread,*

*And yet, however much he slavered,*

*Gained little fame and reward instead!*

*There comes the liberal screamer*

*Returning from his theory.*

*And thinks: To the vulture with writing,*

*I’ll seek my fortune elsewhere, you'll see.*[^67]

The *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter* was one of the most important political newspapers in the 1840s and played an important role in the fight for political reforms, freedom of the press, and German unity, alongside the *Rheinische Zeitung* largely shaped by Karl Marx, Gustav von Struve's *Deutsche Zuschauer*, and Johann Jacoby's *Königsberger Hartungsche Zeitung*.

Exemplary for the political stance of the newspaper is the role of the *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter* in the burgeoning question of a new Rhine border. The French government under Adolphe Thiers tried to launch the idea of the "natural Rhine border," which was to make the Rhine in its entire length the border river between Germany and France. This (unrealistic) demand met with considerable resistance in Germany and led to a wave of anti-French feelings. Soon the focus of the excitement became the *Rheinlied* rhymed by the court clerk Nikolaus Becker, which spread the aggressive refrain *They shall not have it, the free German Rhine* among the people and thereby generated a downright hysterical nationalist mood. The men of the Vormärz, *Young Germany*, who strove for a strong republican state but not necessarily a nationalist one, distanced themselves from this jingoism, and the *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter* published a counter-poem by Robert Prutz, in which he juxtaposes a "free people" against the "free Rhine." Not France, but their own princes stood in the way of "German freedom." That this kind of enlightening press work could be dangerous, Schäfer experienced firsthand. In 1840 and 1841 he was warned by the "Press Police"[^68] and the authority threatened to close the newspaper. The ban of the *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter* was, however, only enforced in December 1845, when Schäfer was already staying at Borobudur to photograph its friezes.

In the light of the career described above, one must describe Adolph Schäfer as one of the important publicists of the Vormärz movement in Saxony, and it is astonishing that the corresponding scientific literature has not sufficiently appreciated this so far.

**Schäfer as a Writer**

Adolph Schäfer was arguably the most essential author of the *Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen* and the successor organ *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter.* Besides that, he published some books, which usually go back to his newspaper contributions. So far, two publications are confirmed:

Schäfer, Adolph, Die Hannoversche Verfassungsfrage bei der Ständeversammlung im Königreich Sachsen. Eine Zusammenstellung der officiellen Documente. Dresden: Walter, 1840.

Schäfer, Adolph, Die wahren Verhältnisse der katholischen Kirche in Sachsen, Dresden, 1840.

**Schäfer and the Reburial of Carl Maria von Weber**

An important cultural-historical and political project must also be mentioned, which was also initiated by Adolph Schäfer. I am speaking of the transfer of the remains of Carl Maria von Weber, composer of the romantic cult opera "Der Freischütz," from London to Dresden. Weber's remains, kept in London under precarious conditions, were to be brought to Dresden to find a dignified resting place. Schäfer turned to various artists of his time and asked them to organize benefit concerts in favor of the project. He also wrote a letter to Felix Mendelssohn-Bartholdy, the most famous musician at that time, and was able to secure his support.[^69]

Quickly the project developed into a national task, which was charged with strong political symbolism. Therefore, the matter was initially fought by the Saxon bureaucracy and made only slow progress. The famous Illustrirte Zeitung from Leipzig reported later about it:

> "In the year 1841, an essay by Dr. Gambihler from Nuremberg appeared in the magazine "Europa", in which he depicted his visit to the Moorfields Chapel with fresh colors and at the end addressed the call to the Germans to give Weber's remains a resting place in patriotic soil. This call was reproduced by other newspapers and finally it passed from the papers for literary entertainment also into the Sächsische Vaterlandsblätter appearing in Dresden at that time, whose editor Adolph Schäfer took up the cause warmly and vividly. At his suggestion, the singing society existing in Dresden under the name: "die Liedertafel" joyfully seized the opportunity to give their darling the first public sign of the deepest sympathy in this regard, and the organization of a concert for this purpose was decided and executed on March 26, 1841, in the most appealing way in the presence of the entire royal court. The proceeds from it amounted to 391 Thlr. and now Schäfer and the bookseller Arnold joined together with the choir master of the Liedertafel Adam into a committee. But the progress did not correspond to this beginning due to several interacting causes, especially since Schäfer later left Dresden entirely, and so this whole matter rested, ..."[^70]

Only in the summer of 1844, when Schäfer had already arrived in Java, did the plan find its realization. The mortal remains of Carl Maria von Weber were transferred by ship from London to Hamburg and then driven up the Elbe to Dresden in a triumphal procession. A dignified ceremony was held at the Catholic Cemetery in Dresden-Friedrichstadt, in which a large crowd of people participated. Gottfried Semper had designed the tomb and Richard Wagner composed the funeral music. Wagner also gave the funeral oration. The widow of Carl Maria von Weber, Caroline von Weber, whose authorized representative for the repatriation of her husband's mortal remains was the advocate Dr. Adolph Schäfer, remembered gratefully the services Schäfer had rendered in this matter. How might the lonely Schäfer in Batavia have felt when he read about this celebration in the newspapers?

**Schäfer and the Saxon Emigration Movement**

When it became foreseeable that Schäfer could not feed his family with the publication of the Sächsische Vaterlandsblätter, he thought intensively about the possibility of emigration to the United States of America.

In the *Wahrheitsfreund*, a German-language newspaper from Cincinnati, Ohio, traces of these Schäferian plans are found on March 31, 1842. It says there:

> "*Mr. Adolf Schäfer wants to found a Saxon colony in the United States of North America under the name Wettin, a new Saxony, to which the progenitor of the Saxon ruling house is to give the name, and invites those of his compatriots who have decided on a settlement in North America to do so. According to the projected prospectus, the Saxon colony is to be founded in one of the old states of North America populated to a large extent by Germans and their descendants to promote its speedy prosperity as much as possible, and multiple offers for the acquisition of lands there are already available. That gross deceptions cannot occur here, as experience with such land trade has taught all too frequently, will be prevented by the lasting cooperation of the local court bookseller, August Bromme, who acquired exact knowledge of the conditions there during his longer stay in America.*
>
> *The Schäfer project, the fruit of the most careful consideration and the strictest consideration of all relevant conditions, primarily envisages a larger tract of land in West Pennsylvania, between Pittsburg and Lake Erie, for the acquisition of which all preliminary steps have already been taken. Situated only 2 to 3 days' journey from the main markets of the Union, in easiest connection with them through canals, highways, and railroads, the settlement there would immediately, or at least after few years, enjoy all the advantages which other colonies in more distant regions can expect only after several decades. Also the climatic and telluric conditions are, according to a credible description, extremely favorable. By the autumn of next year, by the way, the most exact legally documented news about the nature of the tract of land and other important points shall be published here in Dresden by the Colony Bureau (Mr. Bromme). Mr. Schäfer proceeds to America himself in the next spring, and will, under constant correspondence with Mr. Bromme, conduct all business initiating the colonization, whereupon in the spring of 1843 the settlement shall be opened to the participants. Each of the same must take over at least one land parcel, and either be a farmer or practice a trade. Regarding the acceptance of individuals from other classes, the Colony Bureau decides. Each land parcel comprises a land plot of 6 acres and a building plot in the city to be laid out of 60 feet width and 120 feet depth. Furthermore, each participant enjoys an equal claim from the yield of the 3000 acres of Common Lands. The price of a parcel has been set at 50 Thlr. Prussian, payable in installments at judicial disposition. Mr. Schäfer also already communicates the republican constitution of the projected daughter state. (!!)"*[^71]

To what extent this was a real plan or just a figment of the imagination is hard to say, since Saxon emigration to North America, in contrast to emigration from other German regions, is still little researched.[^72]

In another advertisement from the same time, Schäfer presented himself not as an emigrant, but as a kind of agent offering to open the way to the American market for German industrialists. In February 1842, the following advertisement appeared in some German newspapers, including the important Allgemeine Zeitung:

> "*News for merchants, manufacturers and generally for those who stand in business or other connections to the United States of North America, or wish to enter into such. In the month of August or beginning of September of this year, I will start a journey to the United States of North America in colonization matters and stay there for eight to ten months. Should German business people wish to entrust me with orders from their resp. fields in consideration hereof, I will execute the same gladly and conscientiously. I note in this regard that I will visit almost all main places of the Union*."[^73]

Schäfer seems to have still been wrestling with himself—should he emigrate or should he only become an agent for those willing to emigrate and for business people willing to expand? In doing so, he was probably under the influence of his friend Traugott Bromme, with whom he had published the *Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen*. Bromme worked as an emigration consultant and was later to write a whole series of books on this topic. It seems clear that Schäfer was obviously dissatisfied with his life around 1842/43 and was looking for new challenges. His trip to Holland and Java likely stood in causal connection with this life crisis. Another attempt to overcome the crisis was certainly his intense engagement with daguerreotypy.

**Schäfer and Daguerreotypy**

Adolph Schäfer's life between the years 1839 and 1842, as we find it described above, was actually a quite filled one. But just in those years he also occupied himself with daguerreotypy. It was already mentioned that as early as the 2nd number of his *Dresdener Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen* (October 12, 1839), a detailed description of the daguerreotype technique had appeared. We learn everything about plate preparation, its coating, exposure, and fixing. The headline of the article, somewhat unwieldy to our ears, read: *The Daguerreotype*.[^74] This was an extract of an article from the journal *Allgemeine Polytechnisches Journal,* which Adolph Schäfer had perhaps co-authored.[^75]

The first reference to the daguerreotype reached Dresden in a letter that Alexander von Humboldt had written on February 25, 1839, to the Dresden physician, scientist, and artist (student of Caspar David Friedrich) Carl Gustav Carus. In it, Humboldt called the daguerreotype "*one of the most astonishing discoveries of recent times*." Humboldt had learned of the results of Louis Daguerre's work early on in Paris. He was, after all, a member of that scientific commission which was to evaluate Daguerre's invention. But, since Humboldt also knew only the result, but not the process of the procedure, he could say little conclusive about it. Carus may have presented Humboldt's explanations to the King and perhaps discussed them in his salon. The letter was not published. Although Louis Daguerre presented his invention of "drawing with light" to the French Academy of Sciences only on August 19, 1839, various German papers had been printing reports about it since early 1839. In Saxony, too, scientists, opticians, and mechanics occupied themselves with the new technique and made their own attempts.[^76]

Schäfer's early interest in daguerreotypy does not seem to have remained hidden, for already in the early publication on daguerreotypy, "*Praktische Anweisung zum Daguerreotypieren oder die Kunst der Lichtbilder als Porträts, Landschaften, Kupferstiche etc. nach der neuesten Methode sehr schön und in kürzester Zeit hervorzubringen nebst Beschreibung und Abbildung der dazu gehörigen Apparate*"[^77], Dr. Adolph Schäfer from Dresden is mentioned in the afterword, namely as one who has occupied himself with daguerreotypy "*in relation to scientific explorations*."[^78] The leading expert on early photography in Saxony, Professor Jochen Voigt, was able to prove that Adolph Schäfer actually offered himself as a daguerreotypist in Dresden in the summer of 1842. In the Dresdner Anzeiger of June 1, 1842, Voigt found the following advertisement:

> *Daguerreotypy for portraits in at most 30 seconds, daily from early 10 to 1 and afternoon from 3 to 6 o'clock. Premises: Garden Salon of the Hotel "Stadt Wien". Fee for the successful portrait 1 Louis d'or. Groups resp. 8, 10 and 12 Thlr. Registrations are to be effected if possible the day before, in order to be able to determine the hours. Portraits for viewing in the resp. shops of Messrs. Brazzova, Fr. Schulz and Meser on the Schlossgasse. Adolph Schäfer.*[^79]

This advertisement is the decisive clue to the professional change that Adolph Schäfer had been seeking for some time. He was now no longer a chamber secretary, advocate, newspaper publisher, or emigration agent, but a daguerreotypist. Without knowing it, he steered his fate with this decision in a direction that was to lead him first into great adventure and finally into misfortune.

The only question remaining is whether the decision to go to Java was a coincidental one or one influenced by Dresden experiences. The answer to this question cannot be documented by any sources. Certain, however, is that Schäfer had come into contact in Dresden with the Javanese painter Raden Saleh (1811-1880), who stayed in Dresden and Maxen near Dresden between 1839 and 1849 (with interruptions) and maintained close social contacts there.[^80]

**Schäfer and Raden Saleh in Dresden**

When Raden Saleh arrived in Dresden in the autumn of 1839, Schäfer was just about to found his newspaper *Dresdner Wochenblatt für vaterländische Interessen*. Raden Saleh is mentioned in No. 63 and No. 64 of this newspaper (August 5 and 8, 1840). There Adolph Schäfer printed a review of the Dresden Academy Exhibition, which he probably had written himself. Saleh was represented in the exhibition, which had opened on July 15, 1840, with three pictures. Schäfer devotes an astonishing amount of space to these pictures and discusses them in detail. In No. 63 of his paper, he writes at the end of the first part of his review:

> *So we reserve the discussion of two interesting pictures by Raden Saleh from Java, a Lion Hunt and a Sea Storm, (for later)... but remark here preliminarily that these two paintings depict elemental anger, forces and drives of nature...*[^81]

The review in No. 64 makes it clear that Schäfer was very impressed by both the pictures and their painter. Yet he positions himself not as an art connoisseur, but as a critic of the social and political conditions of Saxony. He writes:

> *"The Lion Hunt, oil painting by Raden Saleh from Java, is a very lively composition. Into a large, wild and romantic nature we are led by the energy of the painter, who, born himself in the hot climate, beheld the splendor of the ocean there, the rampant vegetation of tropical lands, and the mountains of volcanic nature. This autopsy [eyewitnessing] also acts on the style of the composition, just as every human being stands not only under the influences of his time but also of his country. On Raden Saleh’s painting, we behold the king of the woods, the lion, already down and overthrown, in death throes and struggle, not far from a rider and steed, both of which he tore down before his fall. Victims of his bestial power, they twitch in their blood, but over the lion himself rears up, attacked by the lioness and excited to fear and rage by her bite, a second steed, from which a lion hunter with manly nerve, brownish skin complexion, strong and wild facial features, in the costume of the East Indian strange to us, strives to slay his and his steed's enemy, the lioness. His strong fist stabs down with the strong iron to bore it into the lioness's brain or eye. On a black steed, himself of dark countenance, a second hunter has galloped up and aims his long pistol at the animal. Other hunters, close to the companions and, like them, firm marrowy natures, prepare for the shot, and a sixth, hunting lust in hot breast, calls to other comrades who, from middle distance and distance, with drawn saber, come galloping in carrière. Everything bold and strong and for the cultivated Europeans, to whom the primal power of nature appears only in their own beards that have recently come into fashion, doubly interesting.*
>
> *A second painting by Raden Saleh, also designed with sure power, shows us a Sea Storm. Ships, whose flags flutter in the wind, fight with the waves, already robbed by those, and almost already their prey. Shipwreck debris rises from the floods. In the airspace the mists or clouds ball up and sweep like black crepes over the shipwreck. The water demon with his foams, pours, swirls, whirlpool heads and sway system is well captured".*[^82]

Since Schäfer did not introduce the Javanese painter in any way, one can assume that Raden Saleh was well known in the Dresden public sphere and did not have to be presented to the audience.

Some months later, on February 16, 1841, Schäfer published in his newspaper, which however was now rechristened *Sächsische Vaterlands-Blätter*, another article in which Raden Saleh played a role. Although the author of the contribution was not mentioned by name, I assume that this article too was written by Schäfer. The central author of the newspaper, Robert Blum, lived in Leipzig and probably did not concern himself closely with Dresden art conditions. The article deals with the acquisition strategy of the Dresden Art Association. The author praised one picture quite particularly, the portrait of Raden Saleh, painted by Carl Bähr. The picture, which caused so much sensation back then, was acquired by the Baltic merchant Friedrich Wilhelm Brederlo in Dresden and hangs today in the Art Museum in Riga. Schäfer wrote:

> *A portrait by Bähr, depicting the Javanese Prince Raden Saleh. We have seen this prince depicted multiple times and always in European clothing: now, after we have seen him again in the picture painted by Bähr in the Oriental costume peculiar and necessary to him, we cannot comprehend how one could ever put an Asian head on the trunk of a European fashion journal, especially since one thereby gave up the advantage of a picturesque costume so carelessly and uselessly. Let the reader, by the way, not be misled by this opening into the erroneous opinion as if the costume were the main thing in the picture painted by Bähr, or as if we considered the same generally to be the main thing in a portrait. The portrait shall give a total impression of the appearance of a human being, thus consider and highlight quite particularly the face and its most excellent expression, the eyes, as the main thing and central point of the particular personal peculiarity, while the costume and other secondary things have only a subordinate, contributing meaning. That the portrait of Prince Saleh painted by Bähr satisfies these requirements of a good, successful portrait emerges certainly and decidedly from the fact that the eye of the viewer, even if occupied for moments with the costume, involuntarily turns again and again to the face and its expression, the eye, and lingers there quite particularly and gladly. The character of the peculiar Oriental facial formation, differing so much from the European one, is captured particularly happily by the painter and reproduced in the picture; the color of the face is warm and transparent to the dark tone conditioned by nature. Also, the painter has avoided, amidst all the seduction of the rich Oriental costume to apply conspicuous shine and splendor, sensibly and prudently every effect-seeking exaggeration in color and arrangement; as indeed Mr. Bähr has already laudably proven in his excellent historical picture purchased by the Art Association "The Anabaptists," what charm and what power nature-true truth and simplicity have over any effect-seeking and exaggeration.*
>
> *If we now grasp the total impression of the discussed picture once more, it bears, although originally a portrait, through the peculiar conception and treatment yet again such a general character that it can rightly be regarded like "The Jewess of Ohr," over which latter it has however essential advantages, as a genre painting and thus should not lie outside the scope of the Art Association.*[^83]

The astonishing thing about this article is that it manages entirely without exoticisms and Orientalist echoes. The Javanese painter is not a representative of an alterity, but a self-evident component of Dresden society. This is an extraordinary and rare approach. In the Dresden literature on Raden Saleh, one is often confronted with a well-meaning but nevertheless inappropriate paternalistic attitude towards the "Javanese Prince." A particularly striking testimony of this kind is found in Ida von Düringsfeld's *In der Heimath. Briefe eines Halbjahres vom Blätterknospen bis zum Blätterfallen.*[^84]

The question arises how well Raden Saleh and Adolph Schäfer knew each other and whether it was Saleh's stories about his homeland Java that awakened the possibility of a Javanese adventure in Schäfer. Unfortunately, we know too little about this to make concrete statements. We also do not know whether Raden Saleh and Schäfer met again later in Batavia. Saleh returned to his homeland in 1852 and lived in Batavia from 1854/55. The possibility that the successful painter and the impoverished photographer met is very likely, especially when one remembers with what intimacy Raden Saleh used to remember his time in Germany later. Besides, Schäfer's address in Batavia in the street Sawa Bezar and Raden Saleh's apartment on Molenvliet were situated only a few meters from each other. Saleh was also interested in photography and probably experimented with photographic recordings himself. Besides photographs of his neo-Gothic house in Cikini/Batavia, which he claimed to have made himself and which he distributed to visitors, he used photographic templates for a number of his painted portraits. A method he had learned in Paris.

The established connection between Raden Saleh, the first painter of Java, and the first photographer of Java, Adolph Schäfer, is new and not yet discussed in Indonesian art history. Its discovery should be an occasion to study the personal and social networks of the two men more closely to find further overlaps. Another question posed by the acquaintance between Raden Saleh and Adolph Schäfer is that of the influence of photography on the development of painting in colonial and in other non-European societies.[^85]

**Summary**

Friedrich Heinrich Adolph Schäfer from Dresden was an enigma for a long time and will probably remain one for a while yet. He was brought close to us by Herrman Moeshart as a colonial phenomenon and has thereby found his place in the international history of photography. In the present contribution, I have tried to integrate further aspects of the life of Adolph Schäfer into his biography. In the future, he will likely no longer be perceived solely as the daguerreotypist who came from the dark, worked for a short time in the light of Java, only to disappear into the darkness of history again. Now we know that he was a trained lawyer and a ministerial secretary. That he founded one of the most important newspapers of the German Vormärz and collaborated with significant men like Robert Blum. He played a certain role in the organization of Saxon emigration to the United States of America and also in the transfer of the mortal remains of Carl Maria von Weber to Dresden. Finally, he was also involved in the positive reception of the work of the Javanese painter Raden Saleh in Dresden. What role Raden Saleh might have played in Schäfer's decision to go to Java must still be considered unexplored.

A better understanding of Adolph Schäfer could be gained if chance willed it and the private correspondence with his family or with Dresden friends could be unearthed. Until then, we will certainly be able to round off the picture with small diligent finds. Whether the enigma that still surrounds him will ever dissolve completely, time will tell.
